#wearecooked

Cookies are like the economy. Everyone participates. Nobody understands it. And that's by design.

Every website you visit drops tracking cookies on your browser. Not 1 or 2. Dozens. Some watch you across 47 different sites. Some live for years. Some send your data over plain HTTP where anyone can intercept it.

The companies setting these cookies spent millions making them invisible. There's no receipt. No notification. No dashboard that says "Google tracked you on 47 sites today, Facebook on 23, Reddit on 12."

They designed it so you wouldn't look. Because if you looked, you'd want to delete them. And if you deleted them, their business model breaks.

Here's the thing — your login cookies and your tracking cookies sit in the same jar. So "clear all cookies" logs you out of everything. That's not a bug. That's the lock on the door.

I built wearecooked to open that door.

It scans every cookie in your browser, classifies them (tracking, advertising, social media, session, preference — 10 categories), scores the worst offenders, and now lets you delete the trackers while keeping your logins intact.

One click. Trackers gone. Still logged into everything.

No data leaves your browser. No account needed. No irony.

Now live on Firefox Add-ons. Chrome Web Store (pending review)

#wearebaked


I visited LinkedIn, Reddit, YouTube, and CNN without signing in. Four sites. My browser talked to 164 other domains I never asked for.

That's not a feature. That's a coke addict snorting your data without permission.

Every page load fires off requests to ad networks, analytics platforms, fingerprinting services, tracking pixels, and beacon endpoints you've never heard of. They don't ask. They don't notify. They just phone home — every few seconds, like clockwork.

I built wearebaked to make all of it visible.

Open the dashboard and watch in real time: every domain your browser contacts, what category it falls into, whether it's first-party or third-party, and which ones are beaconing on a timer. Filter by domain, category, or third-party status. See redirect chains where one click bounces through five tracking domains before reaching the actual page.

Combine it with wearecooked and you get both sides of the picture — cookies AND network traffic. See who's tracking you through cookies, who's tracking you through requests, and clean the trackers without losing your logins.

And because I trust no one with my traffic, I built my own VPN too. WireGuard on my own server. No third-party VPN provider selling "privacy" while logging everything.

Three tools. All free. All open source. All local-first.

- wearecooked — cookie scanner + cleaner
- wearebaked — network traffic dashboard
- privpn — self-hosted VPN

Firefox and Chrome add-ons submitted (pending review). Everything's on GitHub: https://github.com/hamr0

164 domains from four websites. That number should make you uncomfortable. If it doesn't, you haven't been paying attention.


#weareleaking

Close all your tabs. Clear your cookies. Open a fresh browser window.

You're still being tracked.

Cookies get all the attention. But while you were busy clicking "accept cookies" banners, websites were quietly saving tracking data somewhere else on your machine. Somewhere most people don't know exists. No banner. No popup. No permission asked.

I scanned 10 sites I use daily. They left behind 312 pieces of hidden data on my browser. Tracking IDs that follow me across sessions. Encoded blobs I can't even read. Little breadcrumbs from Facebook, Google, and Spotify — placed there without asking.

The best part? Clearing cookies doesn't touch any of it. That's not an oversight. That's the backup plan.

Every major site does this. It's where they keep the stuff they don't want you to find in the cookie jar.

I built weareleaking to find it. It scans every site you visit, shows what hidden data they left behind, flags the suspicious stuff, and tells you how much each site is storing. One dashboard. Every site. Everything they didn't tell you about.

#wearelinked

You click a link on Google. You think you're going straight to the website. You're not.

Your click gets routed through Google first — so they can log exactly what you clicked, when, and where you came from. Then you arrive at the page. Facebook does the same thing. YouTube does it. Outlook does it. Every major platform records your click before they let you leave.

That's just half of it.

Ever notice those long, ugly URLs with a bunch of random characters at the end? Those aren't random. They're tracking tags. They tell the website you're visiting exactly which ad, email, or social media post brought you there. You're basically walking into a store wearing a name tag that says "I came from the Facebook ad" — and you never agreed to put it on.

One system tracks you leaving. The other tracks you arriving. Every click is logged twice.

I built wearelinked to make this visible.

Hover over any link on any page — it shows you where the link actually goes and who's watching the click. Red underlines flag every tracked link. No tech knowledge needed. Just hover and see the truth.

It doesn't block anything. It doesn't break anything. It just shows you what was always there.

All free. All open source. Nothing leaves your browser. Submitted to Chrome + Firefox stores.

#wearewatched

You don't need cookies to be tracked. You don't even need to log in. You don't even need to leave incognito.

Your device is already unique. Your graphics card, your processor, your language settings, even the way your screen draws an invisible image — put it together and it's a fingerprint. More accurate than a cookie. And unlike a cookie, you can't delete it.

Here's how it actually works. You sign into Google once. They capture how your device looks — your screen, your hardware, the way it renders things. Now that fingerprint is tied to your account. Later, you open incognito, don't sign in, and browse. Doesn't matter. Your graphics card didn't change. Your processor didn't change. The fingerprint is the same. They match it back to you.

Incognito hides your history from your roommate. It doesn't hide your device from Google.

Google's ad network sits on roughly 80% of websites. Meta's tracking pixel is on millions more. Data brokers like LiveRamp and Experian buy and sell "identity graphs" — databases that link your anonymous browsing to your real name, email, and purchase history. Your fingerprint is the key that connects all of it.

While that's happening, some sites are also quietly trying to read your clipboard, track your location, or request notification access — often before you've scrolled past the headline. No banner. No opt-out. Most people don't know it's possible.

I built wearewatched to make it visible.

Open any page. Click the extension. See exactly what the site is doing, in plain English. "Drew a hidden image to ID your device." "Read your graphics card info." "Tried to read your clipboard." Each one counted. Each one explained.

Green means clean. Yellow means suspicious. Red means they're profiling your machine.

#weareplayed

"No thanks, I don't want to save money."

You've clicked that button. We all have. It was designed to make you feel stupid for saying no. That's not UX. That's manipulation. And it has a name: confirm-shaming.

It's one of dozens of dark patterns baked into the websites you use every day. Countdown timers that reset when you refresh. "Only 2 left in stock" on a digital product with infinite supply. Pre-checked boxes that sign you up for marketing emails. Unsubscribe links in 8px gray text on a gray background.

These aren't accidents. They're A/B tested, optimized, and deployed at scale. Every major e-commerce site, every SaaS signup flow, every cookie banner — engineered to make you click what they want, not what you want.

The EU calls them deceptive design patterns. Researchers have catalogued hundreds. But nobody built a tool that scores them in real time while you browse.

I did.

weareplayed scans every page you visit and scores it from 0 to 100 based on six categories of manipulation: countdown timers, fake urgency, confirm-shaming, pre-checked consent, hidden opt-outs, and trick questions. Green means clean. Red means you're being played.

No AI. No cloud. Just pattern matching against the page you're looking at, running entirely in your browser.

#wearetosed

You've never read a terms of service in your life. Neither have I. Neither has anyone.

That's the point. They're written to be unreadable. The average privacy policy is 4,000 words of legal fog designed to make your eyes glaze over. Somewhere in paragraph 47, buried in a subordinate clause, is a sentence that says they can sell your data to whoever they want. Somewhere else, another one says you waive your right to sue them. And another that says they can change everything "at any time, without notice."

You clicked "I agree" anyway. We all did. Thousands of times.

Companies know nobody reads these. That's why they put the worst stuff in there. Binding arbitration. Class action waivers. Indefinite data retention. The right to share your data with unnamed "partners." Language so vague it could mean anything — and in court, it will.

I built wearetosed to read them for you.

Open any website. Click the extension. It finds the privacy policy and terms of service automatically, scans every clause against 40+ red flag patterns across six categories — data selling, tracking, indefinite retention, law enforcement access, rights waivers, and unilateral control — and gives you a toxicity score from 0 to 100.

Green means standard boilerplate. Red means you're getting tossed.

No AI. No cloud. Just pattern matching against the document on your screen, running entirely in your browser. It even finds policies that sites try to hide behind JavaScript redirects.

Every "I agree" is a blind handshake. At least now you can see what's in the other hand.

#wearesilent

You're typing your email into a signup form. You haven't clicked submit yet. You're still deciding.

It's already been sent.

Not to the site you're on. To a third-party analytics company you've never heard of. Your email, half-typed, transmitted in real time to a server that has nothing to do with the form you're filling out.

This isn't a hypothetical. A peer-reviewed USENIX study tested the top 100,000 websites and found 2,950 of them leaking form data before the user clicks submit. Session replay tools record every keystroke. Marketing pixels grab your email the moment you type it. Fraud detection scripts read your input fields for "risk scoring." Abandoned cart trackers capture your address before you finish entering it.

You never consented to any of this. There was no banner. No checkbox. No opt-in. They just took it.

I built wearesilent to catch them in the act.

It monitors every form field on every page you visit. When you type, it watches. When a third-party script reads your input and sends it somewhere, the badge turns red. Open the popup — see exactly which field was leaked, what value was sent, and which company received it. "Your email → sent to Meta." "Your address → sent to Loqate." In real time. While you type.

No AI. No cloud. Nothing leaves your browser except the data those scripts were already stealing.

#wearehere

I built eight browser extensions. Each one exposes a different way websites track you. Cookies. Network requests. Fingerprinting. Dark patterns. Hidden storage. Link tracking. Terms of service. Form exfiltration.

Eight extensions. Eight popups. Eight separate scans. That's not privacy awareness — that's a full-time job.

So I built wearehere.

One extension. One click. Every scan runs at once. Cookies, trackers, fingerprinting, pressure tactics, toxic terms, hidden storage, link tracking — all scored into a single risk number from 0 to 100.

Green means clean. Red means leave.

But here's where it gets interesting. wearehere also ships as an MCP server — a plugin for AI assistants like Claude, ChatGPT, or any agent that supports the Model Context Protocol. Give it a URL, get back a full structured privacy audit as JSON. No browser needed. No manual clicking.

That means your AI assistant can now answer: "Is this site safe to sign up for?" And it won't guess. It'll scan the site, check the cookies, read the terms, detect the fingerprinting, and give you an actual answer with evidence.

Privacy analysis as a tool call. One line of config:

```json
{
  "mcpServers": {
    "wearehere": {
      "command": "node",
      "args": ["/path/to/wearehere/mcp-server.js"]
    }
  }
}
```
